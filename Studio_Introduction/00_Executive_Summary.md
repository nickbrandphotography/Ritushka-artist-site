# Executive Summary — The Highest-Converting Art Introduction Document

**Prepared for Ritushka · August 2026**

---

## The question

Which single PDF, sent cold to a decision-maker, has the highest probability of producing a reply and a meeting?

## The answer

**Not a portfolio.**

The winning document is an **8-page A4 Studio Profile built on capability-statement logic** — a document whose job is to make the artist *easy to specify*, not to make the artwork admired.

Its defining features:

| Feature | Why |
|---|---|
| **8 pages, not 30** | Portfolios lose on choice overload and on the reader's time budget. Eight pages is long enough to be credible, short enough to be finished. |
| **Page 2 is a standalone one-pager** | Procurement and design principals scan for roughly six seconds before deciding whether to keep reading. Page 2 must survive that scan alone: what, what size, what price band, how long, how it arrives. |
| **Leads with buyer value, not artist identity** | The buyer is solving a wall, a brief, a budget or a brand problem. "I am an artist" is not a value proposition. "I make the 3-metre piece your joinery wall needs, to your palette, in ten weeks" is. |
| **Answers operational questions early** | Lead time, dimensions, price band, freight, framing, insurance value, trade terms. These are the questions that actually kill deals, and no portfolio answers them. |
| **8–14 images, tightly edited** | Enough to show range and consistency. Not so many that the reader has to choose. |
| **Titled per audience** | "Capability Statement" for corporate and developer procurement. "Studio Profile" for designers, hotels and consultants. "Studio Introduction" for galleries. Same document; the word on the cover changes because the filing cabinet it lands in changes. |

## The single most important structural decision

**Page 2, not page 1, carries the argument.** The cover earns three seconds of attention with one image. Page 2 spends that attention on facts. Everything after page 2 is for the reader who has already decided you might be useful.

Most artist documents invert this: they open with a biography and put the practicalities — if they appear at all — on the last page. That is the single most common and most expensive mistake in the category.

## What gets cut

Removed after research: artist philosophy as a standalone page, printing methods (unless editions are sold), sustainability (unless materially true and specific), long biography, awards and press (unless real and recent), a general "collection overview".

Retained: personal introduction (short, three sentences), why clients commission, a working selection, the commission process, specifications and logistics, and a single closing invitation.

## Sending mechanics — a finding that changes how you use this

Cold-email data across large samples shows emails **with attachments reply at roughly 1.3% versus roughly 2% without** — attachments suppress both replies and deliverability. So:

- **Do not attach this to a first cold email.** Send a four-sentence email with one inline image and a link.
- **Attach it on reply**, when they have asked for something.
- **Hand it over printed** at meetings, fairs and site visits — where the tactile version does work no PDF can do.

The document is a *conversion* asset, not a *prospecting* asset. Used as a prospecting asset it will underperform a plain email.

## Why this outperforms a portfolio

A portfolio asks the reader to do the work: to imagine the piece in the space, to guess the price, to wonder whether you can hit their date. A capability-shaped Studio Profile does that work for them. It converts an aesthetic question ("do I like this?") — which is subjective, deferrable and easy to say no to — into a procurement question ("can this person deliver what my project needs?") — which is objective, urgent and easy to say yes to.
